# 04 — Pre-War Apartment House

> Masonry-clad multiple dwellings, 6–16 stories (occasionally 18–30 with setbacks), c.1900–1940. Harlem/Sugar Hill (409 & 555 Edgecombe Ave, Colonial Parkway Apartments, 555 W 141st), Hamilton Heights, Riverside Drive–West End Ave, Central Park West (the Ardsley, the Eldorado, the Master Building), Grand Concourse Art Deco. Steel or reinforced-concrete frame, limestone base + brick shaft + terra-cotta/metal cap, light courts, flat roof, no street fire escapes.

Real permit case (555 Edgecombe, 1914–16): permit filed for a 12-story building **41.20 m (135 ft 2 in)** tall; the Superintendent of Buildings found the built structure to be 15 stories at **47.70 m (156 ft 6 in)** — over the 45.72 m (150 ft) legal limit — because the sloping site exposes a basement and cellar on Edgecombe Ave. **Rule: on a sloping Harlem/Washington Heights site, add 1–2 exposed levels on the downhill face.**

### Height limit regimes (pick by build year — this drives the silhouette)

**A. 1901–1929 — Tenement House Act (all multiple dwellings, rich or poor)**
- Max height = **1⅓ × street width** (1901), amended 1902 to **1½ × street width** of the *widest* street on which the building has frontage. In force until 1929.
- 60 ft (18.29 m) side street → **27.43 m (90 ft)** → 8–9 stories.
- 100 ft (30.48 m) avenue → **45.72 m (150 ft)** → 12 stories (pre-WWI) or 15–16 stories (1920s).
- **Flat top, no setbacks.** Every 1902–1929 apartment house on an avenue rises sheer to the cap and stops. This is why West End Ave / Riverside Dr / Grand Concourse read as a table-flat cornice line.
- Every room must have a window on a street, rear yard, or light court → the court plans in §4.
- Over 6 stories ⇒ fireproof construction required ⇒ **no fire escapes** (see §7).

**B. 1916 Zoning Resolution (applies to hotels, apartment hotels, commercial; residential only after 1929)**

| Height district | Street wall height | Setback slope above street wall | 18.29 m (60 ft) street | 30.48 m (100 ft) street |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 times | 1.0 × street width | 1 ft in per **2 ft** up (1:2) | 18.29 m | 30.48 m |
| 1¼ times | 1.25 × | 1:2.5 | 22.86 m | 38.10 m |
| 1½ times | 1.5 × | 1:3 | 27.43 m | 45.72 m |
| 2 times | 2.0 × | 1:4 | 36.58 m | 60.96 m |
| 2½ times | 2.5 × | 1:5 | 45.72 m | 76.20 m |

- Setback slope generalizes as **1 unit horizontal per (2 × district multiplier) units vertical**.
- **25% tower exemption:** above the setback envelope a tower may rise to unlimited height if its footprint is ≤ **0.25 × lot area**. The Eldorado's two 12-story towers sit on ≈462 m² each out of a 3,694 m² lot (25.0%).
- Residential Manhattan avenues were predominantly **1½-times** districts.

**C. 1929 Multiple Dwelling Law — residential brought under zoning**
- Height no longer capped; extra stories permitted **if setbacks are provided**. In practice setbacks begin at the height of the cornices of the pre-1929 neighbours: **41 – 46 m (135 – 150 ft)**.
- Post-1929 buildings on West End Ave / Riverside Dr: 18–19 floors of apartments plus penthouses, 3–5 setback levels.

### Per-floor differences (checklist for the generator)
1. Ground floor: taller, stone-clad, rusticated, larger openings, entrance + service door + basement grilles.
2. 2nd floor: often the "piano nobile" — stone or brick-rusticated, wrought-iron window railings (555 Edgecombe), sometimes columns between openings, balustraded balcony over the entrance.
3. Shaft floors 3 → n−2: identical. **Only** variation is scattered stone balconettes.
4. Balconette floors: real examples place stone/terra-cotta balconettes at the **5th, 8th, 10th and 11th** stories (555 Edgecombe: rounded terra-cotta balconies on brackets at the 5th and 10th, rectangular ones at the 8th and 11th; 2745 Broadway: stone balconettes at the 5th, 8th and 10th). Rule: 2–4 balconette floors, roughly every 2.5–3 stories, on the centre bay and/or the 2 flanking bays only.
5. Cap floors: contrasting brick patterning, terra-cotta panels, double-height piers, arched heads.
6. Top floor: sometimes taller; the setback/penthouse floors on post-1929 buildings are set back and get French doors + terrace railings.

## 4. COURTYARDS & COURT PLANS

Driven by the 1901 Tenement House Act ("every room, including bathrooms and kitchens, must have a window on a street, rear yard, or light court") and after 1929 by the Multiple Dwelling Law.

### Plan-type weights
| Plan | Weight | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Rectangular block + central interior light court** | 0.24 | 555 Edgecombe. Court invisible from the street; a dark slot in plan |
| **Side light courts (1–3 notches in one street facade)** | 0.26 | 2745 Broadway: "W 105th St facade divided into three wings by two light courts" |
| **H-plan** (courts front and rear) | 0.14 | Two street-facing courts on opposite faces |
| **U-plan** (single court open to street) | 0.13 | "Two pavilions around central light court" |

| **Full entrance court** (grand courtyard, arcade entry) | 0.05 | Dakota / Apthorp / Graham Court class; entry through a vaulted arcade |
| **Plain rectangular block, no court** (narrow site) | 0.10 | ≤15.2 m (50 ft) frontage |

### Court dimensions

**Pre-1929 (Tenement House Act practice)**
| Court type | Width | Depth |
|---|---|---|
| Side/inner light court, narrow site | 1.5 – 3.0 m (5 – 10 ft) | 6.1 – 15.2 m (20 – 50 ft) |
| Side light court, avenue building | 3.0 – 7.6 m (10 – 25 ft) | 7.6 – 18.3 m (25 – 60 ft) |
| Central interior light court | 4.6 – 9.1 m (15 – 30 ft) | 6.1 – 12.2 m (20 – 40 ft) |
| Street-facing entrance court (U/H) | 7.6 – 18.3 m (25 – 60 ft) | 4.6 – 12.2 m (15 – 40 ft) |
| Grand entrance courtyard | 18.3 – 30.5 m (60 – 100 ft) | 18.3 – 30.5 m (60 – 100 ft) |

**1929 Multiple Dwelling Law statutory minimums (use these for post-1929 buildings)**
| Rule | Value |
|---|---|
| Inner court min. width | **0.333 m of width per 1.0 m of building height** (4 in per ft), never less than **4.57 m (15 ft)** |
| Inner court min. area | 2 × (width)², never less than **32.5 m² (350 sq ft)**; requirement capped at 111.5 m² (1,200 sq ft) |
| Inner court, buildings ≤3 stories | 0.25 m per 1.0 m of height, min width 3.05 m (10 ft), min area 23.2 m² (250 sq ft) |
| Outer court, if < 9.14 m (30 ft) wide | width ≥ **2 × depth** |
| Outer court, if ≥ 9.14 m (30 ft) wide | width ≥ depth |
| Outer court max width required | 18.29 m (60 ft) |
| Rear yard, first 38.10 m (125 ft) of height | 9.14 m (30 ft) |
| Rear yard, above 38.10 m | 15.24 m (50 ft); towers exempt |
| Side yard, if provided | 2.44 – 9.14 m (8 – 30 ft) |

Worked example: a 45.72 m (150 ft) tall building needs an inner court **15.24 m (50 ft)** wide. This is why grand courtyard buildings exist and why post-1929 blocks are U- and H-shaped rather than doughnuts.

### Court treatment (visual)
- **Light court** — utilitarian: pale yellow or common brick, plain rectangular openings, stone or terra-cotta sills only, no belt courses, exposed vent pipes, conduit, cables, sometimes a wire mesh across the top. Primary-facade material returns only **0.3 – 1.5 m (1 – 5 ft)** around the reveal, then switches to cheap brick.
- **Entrance court** — designed: primary brick and trim continue, the entrance enframement sits at the back wall, brick or stone paving, historic iron fence + gate at the street line (h 1.07 – 1.52 m), planting beds, 1–2 lamps.
- Courts are where **retrofit fire escapes** appear (one Riverside Dr building: "Fire escapes in light court installed in 1929").

## 7. FIRE ESCAPES — THE SINGLE MOST-GOTTEN-WRONG DETAIL

The 1901 Tenement House Act required **fireproof construction for every multiple dwelling over six stories**. Fireproof buildings have enclosed fire stairs and therefore **no exterior fire escapes**. Dolkart, surveying every building on West End Avenue: *"none of the tall apartment buildings on West End Avenue built after 1901 have fire escapes."*

| Case | P(street-facing fire escape) | P(court/rear fire escape) |
|---|---|---|
| ≥7 stories, built 1902–1940 | **0.02** | 0.15 (retrofit, in the light court) |
| 6 stories, built 1902–1929 (non-fireproof flats) | 0.35 | 0.55 |
| 6 stories, built ≤1901 (Old Law) | 0.85 | 0.60 |
| ≥7 stories, built ≤1901 | 0.55 | — |

If a fire escape is generated: balcony 0.91 – 1.07 m deep × 1.83 – 2.44 m wide; drop-ladder or 45° stair; flat-bar/angle frame 0.05 × 0.008 m; railing h 0.76 – 0.91 m; anchored below sill level; **paint colour black, dark red-brown, or silver-aluminium**.

## DETAILS CHECKLIST
Ordered by what makes it read as an authentic NYC pre-war apartment house at 30 m.

1. **The flat, hard top at exactly 45.72 m (150 ft) with a heavy cornice.** No setbacks, no pitch, no crown. Twelve to sixteen stories rising sheer and stopping. Cornice projection **0.91 – 1.83 m** — it casts a 0.9 m black shadow band across the top two stories. This one silhouette does 40% of the work.
2. **The base/shaft/cap tonal split.** Two-to-three stories of pale limestone (`#C0B6A2`), then 9–12 stories of brick, then 1–2 stories of patterned brick + terra cotta. Never one material top to bottom.
3. **Window reveals of 0.14 m** with a projecting sill (0.04 – 0.08 m) and a visible lintel. Every opening is a recessed box with three shadowed returns.
4. **The through-wall AC grid.** 0.66 × 0.42 m sleeves centred under 30 – 55% of sills, in vertical columns, with a rust streak below each. Nothing dates a building to "New York, now" faster.
5. **Bay rhythm at 3.35 m on centre** with a wider centre bay for the entrance, 7–11 bays on a 30.5 m frontage. Uniform spacing with no entrance emphasis is wrong.
6. **The court notch.** A 3 – 7.6 m wide, 7.6 – 18.3 m deep vertical slot cut into the street facade (or two of them), with pale yellow brick and no ornament inside it. Prewar apartments are not solid boxes.
7. **Wooden water tank on steel legs** on the roof — P = 0.85 above 7 stories. Tank dia 3.0 – 4.6 m, top of tank 8 – 12 m above the parapet. It must break the skyline.
8. **NO fire escapes on the street facade** for anything ≥7 stories built after 1901. P = 0.02. This is the most common single error.
9. **Terra-cotta belt courses at 2–4 heights**, 0.30 – 0.60 m tall, projecting 0.05 – 0.15 m, in white/cream glaze at roughness 0.45 — bright horizontal lines against the brick, each carrying a soot streak below.
10. **The entrance event:** a 2.44 – 4.88 m wide, 3.05 – 5.49 m tall stone surround with an arch + keystone or columns + entablature, a balustraded balcony above it, and a canvas canopy running 4.57 – 7.62 m to within 0.61 m of the curb at 2.44 – 2.74 m clear height.
11. **Tripartite window groups** — 22% of bays get a triple opening 2.44 – 3.05 m wide with 0.10 – 0.20 m mullions (the living-room bay). Not all bays identical.
12. **Clay-tile coping** on the parapet, `#B0763F`, 0.05 – 0.10 m tall, projecting 0.03 – 0.05 m each side, with visible 0.30 – 0.40 m joints.
13. **Scattered stone balconettes** at 2–4 floors only (typ 5th, 8th, 10th, 11th), centre bay, on brackets, projecting 0.30 – 0.76 m.
14. **Granite water table** 0.15 – 0.45 m above the sidewalk with iron-grilled basement windows below it.
15. **Cornice removed on 1 in 3 buildings** — flat brick parapet, a mismatched brick scar band, and orphaned terra-cotta brackets left behind.
16. **Iron window railings on the second floor** only, 0.61 – 0.84 m tall, bar dia 0.013 – 0.019 m.
17. **Building sits on the sidewalk line** — 0 – 1.5 m setback, no areaway, no stoop. This is the primary difference from the rowhouse.
18. Elevator bulkhead 3.66 – 5.49 m + stair bulkhead 2.44 – 4.01 m + chimney, clustered, not centred.
19. Pale yellow secondary/rear elevations with a plain window grid and zero ornament.
20. Diaperwork or contrasting-brick patterning confined to the cap stories.

## COMMON MISTAKES

1. **Setbacks on a 1902–1929 building.** Under the Tenement House Act the building rises **sheer** to 1.5 × street width and stops flat. Setbacks appear only on post-1929 residential (18+ stories) and on pre-1929 *hotels*. A stepped ziggurat on a 1915 apartment house is anachronistic.
2. **Wrong height cap.** 100 ft (30.48 m) avenue ⇒ **45.72 m (150 ft)**; 60 ft (18.29 m) side street ⇒ **27.43 m (90 ft)**. Not "whatever looks tall."
3. **Diminishing floor heights.** That is the rowhouse rule. Pre-war apartment shaft floors are **uniform** (2.90 – 3.35 m); only the ground floor is tall (3.66 – 4.57 m).
4. **Fire escapes on the street facade.** Almost never on ≥7 stories post-1901 — fireproof construction was mandatory, so there are enclosed stairs instead. Put them in the light court if you need them at all.
5. **Flush windows.** Recess 0.10 – 0.20 m and model the jamb, head and sill returns. This is the #1 CG tell on any masonry building.
6. **Cornice too thin or missing entirely.** A 0.15 m fascia is not a cornice. Projection **0.91 – 1.83 m** with brackets at 0.91 – 1.52 m centres and a 200–300-unit dentil band. If you delete the cornice (33% of the stock), you must add the mismatched brick scar band and orphaned brackets — a clean flat top is wrong either way.
7. **Solid rectangular block, no courts.** 90% of pre-war apartment houses have at least one light court, because the 1901 law required a window in every room. Cut 1–3 notches 3 – 7.6 m wide and 7.6 – 18.3 m deep into the street facade.
8. **Court walls in the good brick.** Courts and party walls switch to pale yellow or common brick, lose all ornament, and get 2–3× the patching. Continuing the beige brick and terra-cotta belt courses into a light court is a giveaway.
9. **No air conditioners.** Through-wall sleeves 0.66 × 0.42 m on 30 – 55% of openings, in vertical columns below the sills. A pre-war NYC apartment building with a clean facade does not exist.
10. **One brick colour, one brick tone.** Add ±6 – 12% per-brick lightness jitter, and use a *named* colour: beige `#CDBFA6`, tan `#C6AC81`, red `#9E4B36`. Not generic brick-red `#B22222`.
11. **Uniform bays across the whole frontage.** The entrance bay is 1.15 – 1.60 × wider. Corners get a 0.91 – 1.83 m end pier. 22% of bays are triple-width.
12. **Terra cotta rendered as matte stone.** Glazed architectural terra cotta is the only glossy element on the facade — roughness **0.30 – 0.50**. Matte terra cotta kills the belt-course highlight that says "pre-war."
13. **Modern one-piece picture windows.** Even replacements are 1/1 double-hung within the *original* masonry opening (0.86 – 1.14 m wide). Widening openings to make ribbon windows is a post-war move.
14. **Brick course scale.** Course height **0.0667 m**; 3 courses = 0.203 m. Bricks that read as 0.10 m tall halve the apparent building size.
15. **Cornice line broken per building unit.** Within a blockfront of contemporaneous buildings the cornice heights are *nearly* equal (all at the same statutory cap) but not identical — vary by ±0.6 – 1.5 m, and never align them perfectly.
16. **Stoop and areaway.** Pre-war apartment houses have neither. 0–5 risers at most, and the facade is on the property line.
17. **Water tank omitted, or made metal.** NYC roof tanks are **wood-stave on a steel frame**, dia 3.0 – 4.6 m, and they are present on 85% of buildings above 7 stories. Steel tanks are the exception.
18. **Balconettes on every floor.** They appear on 2–4 floors only, roughly every 2.5–3 stories, and only on the centre and flanking bays.
19. **Symmetric Art Deco.** The 1930s variant is asymmetric — off-centre entrance, rounded bay on one side, corner windows on one corner only.
20. **Clean limestone base.** Limestone at street level soils heavily: darken the bottom 1.0 m by 15 – 30% and inside corners by 20 – 35%, and add 5 – 30% parged patching.
21. **Ignoring the sloping-site rule.** On Edgecombe Ave, Riverside Dr, Hamilton Heights and Sugar Hill, the downhill face exposes 1–2 extra full-height basement levels in granite, so the same building is 12 stories on one street and 14 on the other.
22. **Roman brick modelled at standard length.** Where a Roman-brick facade is called for, the unit is **0.295 m (11.625 in)** long with a deeply raked 0.013 – 0.019 m joint — a very different, more horizontal texture.
