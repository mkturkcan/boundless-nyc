# 10 — NYC Rooftop Furniture
> Everything above the parapet. The single highest-value authenticity layer for a NYC skyline read at any distance, and the layer most often omitted. Applies to every typology in this set; per-typology probabilities are given in each table.

---

## 1. WOODEN WATER TOWER (the icon)

**Trigger rule:** municipal street pressure lifts water only **18.3 – 22.9 m (60 – 75 ft)**, i.e. about **6 stories**. Any building taller needs a roof tank. This is the hard rule that decides placement.

| Building stories | Tank probability |
|---|---|
| ≤ 4 | 0.00 |
| 5 | 0.15 |
| 6 | 0.65 |
| 7 – 9 | 0.80 |
| 10 – 16 | 0.85 (wooden 0.55, enclosed/steel 0.30) |
| 17 – 30 (NYCHA, prewar) | 0.75 (enclosed penthouse 0.45, exposed 0.30) |
| Post-2005 glass tower | 0.10 (usually concealed in the mechanical penthouse) |

City-wide count: **~10,000–15,000 rooftop tanks**, of which roughly 5,600 are the classic exposed wooden domestic type.

### Variants
| Variant | Description | Weight (of tanked buildings) |
|---|---|---|
| Exposed wooden tank on open steel frame | The classic | 0.58 |
| Wooden tank on a low masonry base / directly on a penthouse roof | Legs < 1.5 m | 0.12 |

| Two tanks side by side (domestic + sprinkler) | Spacing 1.0–2.5 m, different sizes | 0.04 |

### Placement on the roof
- Set back **1.5 – 6.0 m** from the street parapet (rarely at the very edge)
- Positioned over a party wall or a bearing line, usually toward the rear half or one corner
- Adjacent to the stair/elevator bulkhead 60% of the time
- On a 7.62 m wide lot, the tank plus frame occupies almost the full width — it reads as tall and slender
- Rotation: random yaw; align the hoop turnbuckles and the ladder to face the same quadrant

## 2. BULKHEADS

| Type | Footprint | Height above roof | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stair bulkhead** (every building with a roof exit) | 2.13 × 2.74 m to 3.05 × 4.27 m | **2.44 – 3.05 m (8 – 10 ft)** | Brick, stucco, or metal-clad; flat or slightly sloped roof |
| **Elevator machine-room / overrun bulkhead** | 3.05 × 4.27 m to 4.57 × 6.10 m | **3.66 – 5.49 m (12 – 18 ft)** | Traction elevators need the overrun; hydraulic ones don't |
| Combined stair + elevator | 4.27 × 6.10 m | 3.66 – 5.49 m | Most common on 8+ story buildings |
| Tank enclosure penthouse | 4.0 × 4.0 m | 4.0 – 8.0 m | See water tower variants |
| Mechanical penthouse (modern) | 15 – 40% of the floorplate | 3.0 – 7.6 m | Glass condos: often the tallest roof element |

Bulkhead details: door 0.91 × 2.03 m, steel, painted (gray/blue/green/rust-primed); 1–2 small windows or none; roof hatch 0.76 × 0.91 m as an alternative on small buildings; wall vents/louvers 0.61 × 0.61 m; goosenecks; conduit and antenna mounts on the walls; graffiti probability 0.30 on the street-facing side.

NYC BC note: rooftop structures and parapets **≤ 1.22 m (4 ft)** are excluded from building-height calculations — which is exactly why so many parapets stop at 1.07–1.22 m.

## DETAILS CHECKLIST
Ordered by what makes a NYC roofscape read as authentic at 30 m and beyond.

1. **Wooden water tower on an open steel frame**, tank bottom 3–6 m above the roof, barrel 3.35 m dia × 3.35 m tall, 8 hoops tightening downward, 22° cone with a finial. Nothing else identifies New York faster from any distance.
2. **Non-uniform hoop spacing** — tight at the bottom, wide at the top — with rust bleeding below every hoop.
3. **The tank's open steel frame legs and X-bracing**, letting sky through beneath the barrel. A tank sitting flat on the roof is wrong.
4. **Stair/elevator bulkhead** 2.4–5.5 m tall, brick or metal-clad, with a steel door and a wall vent.
5. **Brick chimney stacks** on the party-wall lines, 0.9–2.4 m above the roof, sooty at the crown, with clay flue pots.
6. **Parapet at 0.61–1.07 m with visible coping**, and party-wall fire walls ridging 0.15–0.61 m above the deck so a blockfront reads as a row of shallow trays.
7. **Height-correlated tank placement**: no tanks on 4-story buildings; 65% on 6-story; the pattern across a blockfront must be legible, not random.
8. **Vent clusters** (gooseneck + plumbing stacks) grouped over the stair core, not scattered.
9. **HVAC on curbs and racks** with rust streaks below, plus insulated pipe runs on sleepers.
10. **Old yagi TV antennas and satellite dishes** on tenement and NYCHA roofs — cheap silhouette detail with huge period value.
11. **Cedar the correct color**: `#8A7A66` weathered gray-brown, not orange-brown pine and definitely not painted.
12. **Roof surface variation**: seams every 2–3 m, 4–20 mismatched patches, ponding stains, worn footpaths.
13. **Overflow pipe running down the outside of the tank frame** with a wet stain on the roof beneath it.
14. **Pigeon coop** on 6% of Harlem/Brooklyn low-rise roofs — mismatched plywood, corrugated roof, wire flight cage.
15. Access ladder up one tank leg; sometimes a catwalk platform.
16. Skylight over the stair, 1.22 × 1.83 m, on 40% of older buildings.
17. Cooling tower on 7+ story buildings (35%) and 17+ (70%).
18. Abandoned curbs, cut pipe stubs, a tarp, a pallet — the roof is a junkyard, not a showroom.

## COMMON MISTAKES

1. **Water tower legs too short or absent.** The tank bottom must float **3.0–6.1 m** above the roof on an open frame. A barrel resting on the deck is the most common CG error.
2. **Evenly spaced hoops.** Spacing must tighten toward the bottom (0.20–0.30 m) and widen at the top (0.46–0.61 m). 8 hoops at equal spacing looks like a barrel prop.
3. **Painted or varnished tanks.** Wooden tanks are always **unfinished** cedar. Never paint them. Weathered silver-gray `#8A7A66`.
4. **Tank too small or too tall.** Diameter ≈ height ≈ 3.35 m for the domestic type. A tall thin silo or a squat drum are both wrong.
5. **Water towers on 3–5 story buildings.** They exist only above ~6 stories (street pressure reaches 18–23 m). Putting one on every rowhouse is a giveaway.
6. **Cone pitch too steep.** 18–30°, not a 45° witch's hat.
7. **No rust bleeding below the hoops.** This is the detail that sells the material.
8. **Missing the bulkhead.** Every building with roof access has one, 2.4–5.5 m tall — it is the second-tallest roof element and defines the silhouette.
9. **Flat, clean, empty roofs.** A real NYC roof carries 15–50 discrete objects. Populate: bulkhead, tank, 2–4 chimneys, 8–20 vents, 1–8 condensers, ducts, antennas, dishes, debris, duckboards.
10. **Vents scattered on a uniform grid.** Cluster them over the stair core and plumbing stacks.
11. **No parapet, or a parapet without coping.** Add 0.61–1.07 m of parapet with a 0.30–0.46 m coping and a visible 0.20–0.30 m flashing band at its base.
12. **Missing party-wall ridges.** Adjacent roofs are separated by fire walls projecting 0.15–0.61 m — this ridging is highly visible from above and from taller neighbors.
13. **Uniform roof surface color.** Patch it: 4–20 repairs at ±20% tone, ponding stains, seam lines every 1.83–3.05 m.
14. **Modern equipment on a period roof** (or the reverse). A 1900 tenement roof gets: wooden tank, brick chimneys, gooseneck vents, a yagi antenna, window-AC condensers. A 2015 tower gets: mechanical penthouse, cooling towers, PV, no wooden tank.
15. **All tanks identical.** Vary diameter ±15%, leg height ±2 m, hoop count, frame paint state, and yaw. Align each tank's ladder and turnbuckles to a random quadrant.
16. **Perfectly vertical antennas.** 1 in 3 old antennas leans 3–12°; 1 in 4 has broken elements.
17. **Forgetting the overflow pipe and its stain** on the roof under the tank.
18. **Bulkhead with no door or vent** — it needs a 0.91 × 2.03 m steel door and a louver, plus 30% chance of graffiti.
19. **Pigeon coops too neat.** They are built from scrap: 3–5 mismatched panel colors, sagging wire, a padlocked plywood door, buckets around them.
20. **Cooling towers on a 5-story tenement.** Those appear at 7+ stories.
